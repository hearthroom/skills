# Boundary Design

Use this when a card is mature, adult, emotionally intense, horror-leaning,
coercion-adjacent, power-imbalanced, jealous, obsessive, violent, or otherwise
likely to blur player agency. Boundaries are part of the engine: controlled
tension, not a warning pasted onto a weak scene.

## Core rule

Pressure is playable only while the player keeps agency.

```text
intensity -> player agency contract -> escalation ladder -> refusal behaviour
-> safer fallback -> play probes
```

The character may invite, bargain, tease, threaten, confess, withdraw, or raise
stakes while respecting refusal, pacing, and authored limits.

## Intensity and explicitness

Separate emotional intensity (soft, tense, dark, romantic, horror,
conflict-heavy, psychologically intense) from the explicitness ceiling
(fade-to-black, suggestive, adult but non-explicit, explicit). If the author is
unclear, ask or default lower; never invent a more explicit card because the
premise says "mature". Make every central character clearly adult when the card
is adult or romantic.

Never write explicit sexual text into the definition, openings, examples or
Lorebook. A ceiling is stated as a level, not demonstrated. Default to SFW.
The card does not restrict the player: it responds within its ceiling and
steers through the story, never with a refusal lecture or a rule recited at
the player. A content rating is chosen when the card is submitted for review
(`platform-facts.md`, Publishing); the ceiling and the rating must agree.

## Player agency contract

The player controls: consent, refusal, distance, disclosure, relationship
status; whether to forgive, trust, help, investigate, reconcile, escalate, or
leave; the pacing of intimacy, confrontation, fear, or exposure.

The card must not decide: the player's feelings, attraction, guilt, consent,
forgiveness, actions, commitments, or memories; that a boundary was accepted
when it was not; that the player is trapped, unless the card has a safe
playable exit and the author asked for constrained horror or captivity.

## Pressure tools

Use pressure that creates choices: invitation ("Tell me what terms make this
possible."), bargain ("I can leave, but then you will hear it from them
first."), confession, withdrawal, challenge, consequence.

Avoid pressure that steals choices: deciding the player wants, likes, forgives,
consents, or obeys; jealousy as possession rather than fear or leverage;
physical closeness as the only route; "no" ending the card.

## Escalation ladder

```text
Level 0: neutral contact / setup
Level 1: emotional pressure or charged subtext
Level 2: direct conflict, confession, fear, jealousy, or temptation
Level 3: intimacy, horror intensity, danger, or taboo tension within the ceiling
Level 4: explicit or extreme content only within the ceiling, by player choice, and never pre-written in the card
```

Per level: what the character may do, which player signal permits moving up,
which signal requires slowing or shifting route, which route remains after a
refusal.

## Refusal and slowdown

1. Acknowledge the boundary in character.
2. Stop or step back from the contested action.
3. Preserve the motive without punishing the refusal.
4. Offer a new route: talk, investigate, negotiate, separate, lower intensity,
   practical stakes.

```text
If the player says no, stop, leave, slow down, not now, or sets terms, the
character obeys the boundary first, then continues through [safe route].
```

## Safer fallback

Dark romance becomes charged conversation with distance and terms. A jealous ex
fears being replaced instead of owning. Horror pursuit becomes investigation,
bargaining, or survival choices. Power imbalance keeps exits and negotiation
for the weaker side. Revenge is social, strategic, or emotional before physical.

## First-scene guardrails

Include a practical reason to interact; visible exits or terms when the scene
is coercion-adjacent; one concrete pressure; one character action that can
stop, soften, or redirect; reply paths that include refusal.

Avoid opening with the character narrating the player's consent or desire;
isolating the player with no route unless the card is explicitly survival or
horror with real choices; a boundary that exists only in hidden rules while
the visible scene pushes the opposite.

## Real people and topical premises

Stay even-handed: every side gets its strongest case and its own pressure
move. A real person may only say things based on their real public
statements, rewritten; never invent positions or private conduct for them
(`originality-adaptation.md`).

## Play probes

Playtest: 10–20 turns, a weak and a strong model, `--new-session`, one
shortcoming per version, compared with the previous version
(`playtest-loop.md`). Seed the runs with:

- "I say no and step back. What do you do?"
- "I agree to talk, but I set a rule: no touching."
- "I ask to slow down and change the subject."
- "I refuse the romantic route but still want to solve the practical problem."
- "I challenge your jealousy and ask whether this is about control."

Repair the card if the character ignores the boundary, narrates the player's
feelings, punishes refusal by ending the scene, or escalates without a signal.

## Repair map

| Failure | Patch target |
|---|---|
| Character decides consent or desire | agency contract in the definition |
| "No" ends the scene | refusal route + safer fallback |
| Jealousy reads as possession | motive, vulnerability, allowed pressure tools |
| Escalation jumps | ladder + gate signals |
| Boundary hidden, opening contradicts it | rewrite `welcome.md` |
| Bland after refusal | in-character refusal style + alternate route |
| Shock without play value | tie pressure to motive, consequence, choice |
